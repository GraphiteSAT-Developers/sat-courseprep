# Build a verified Desmos tutor before writing lessons

The project is feasible and has a real opening, but it should be built in a specific order. **The legal question of whether Gemini may be used at all comes first.** Both the Gemini API terms and Google Cloud's generative-AI terms bar services "directed towards or likely to be accessed by" people under 18, and most SAT takers are 16–17. Only after that question is settled should the team build anything students see. The core asset is a **verified Desmos engine**: a headless instance of the official Desmos API that the AI must use to solve and check every item before it tutors a student. The course content around it is a library of about 13 core Desmos moves, each paired with a by-hand fallback, plus a bank of original SAT-style items. Fine-tuning is not a usable lever right now. The Gemini Developer API offers no tuning, and Vertex tuning covers only the 2.5 models that platform retires on October 20, 2026. So "training" the AI on Desmos means four things: tool use, retrieval of a curated technique library, rejection-sampled example traces that executed correctly, and a regression eval suite that gates every release. The competitive case holds up. Free AI SAT prep now comes from Khan, Google and The Princeton Review, but no product was found whose AI actually operates a Desmos graph. A Desmos-first product aimed at 700–800 scorers, which verifies its own answers by computation, is a defensible niche. Three blockers must clear before launch. College Board refuses permission to use its questions with generative AI, so every item must be original. The free Desmos API tier forbids production use. And Gemini's age clause applies. All findings below come from web-search summaries, because direct page fetches were blocked, so the items in the final verification table need checking by hand before money is spent.

## An 800 tolerates roughly one miss, and Bluebook's Desmos is the tool students will actually have

The Digital SAT Math section has **44 questions in 70 minutes**, split into two 35-minute modules of 22 questions. In each module, **20 questions are scored and 2 are unscored pretest items** that students cannot identify, which leaves about 1.6 minutes per question ([College Board](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/sat/whats-on-the-test/structure); [test spec](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/digital-sat-test-spec-overview.pdf)). The test is multistage adaptive. Module 1 performance sends the student to an easier or harder Module 2, and scores come from item response theory rather than a raw count ([Assessment Framework](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/assessment-framework-for-digital-sat-suite.pdf)). Only the harder Module 2 can produce an 800. Third-party guides put the cap on the easier route somewhere between about 560 and 650, though none of those figures is official ([PrepScholar](https://blog.prepscholar.com/how-many-can-you-miss-for-a-perfect-score-on-the-sat); [Lumist](https://www.lumist.ai/blog/digital-sat-scoring-adaptive-testing-explained)). College Board publishes no conversion table. Prep-company estimates say an 800 allows **0–2 misses on the hard route, depending on the form**, and Sigma Prep advises planning for zero ([PrepScholar](https://blog.prepscholar.com/how-many-can-you-miss-for-a-perfect-score-on-the-sat); [Sigma Prep](https://www.sigmaprep.io/sat-math-how-many-can-you-miss)). This shapes the whole course. Under IRT, a careless miss on an easy Module 1 item does double damage: it costs more than a hard miss, and it risks routing the student onto the capped path. An 800 course therefore has to treat checking on easy items as seriously as content on hard ones. A fast Desmos check of a by-hand answer is the cheapest form of insurance.

Content is split across four domains: **Algebra (~35%), Advanced Math (~35%), Problem-Solving & Data Analysis (~15%) and Geometry & Trigonometry (~15%)**. Every module draws from all four ([College Board Math specs](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/k12-educators/about/alignment/math)). That puts about 70% of scored items in exactly the domains where Desmos does the most work. Most questions are four-option multiple choice. The rest are student-produced responses (SPR), whose entry rules are themselves a scoring hazard. Students get **5 characters for a positive answer and 6 for a negative one**, no %, $ or commas, and decimals that are too long must be truncated or rounded at the fourth digit ([Bluebook directions](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/english-sat-test-directions-bb.pdf)). An entry like 0.67 for 2/3 is marked wrong ([Target Test Prep](https://sat.blog.targettestprep.com/fractions-to-decimals/)). The AI's final-answer checker needs to enforce these rules in code.

Bluebook embeds a **College Board-configured Desmos graphing calculator and a scientific calculator on every math question**, and content does not carry over between the two ([Desmos Help](https://help.desmos.com/hc/en-us/articles/30913914831757); [Bluebook tools](https://bluebook.collegeboard.org/students/tools)). According to Desmos's College Board reference PDF, the test version **disables images, folders and notes, and automatically turns on Log Mode for applicable exponential, logarithmic and power regressions** ([official CB Desmos PDF](https://www.desmos.com/assessment-pdfs/CollegeBoard_Desmos_Calculator.pdf)). One prep guide claims the test version is not stripped down, which contradicts that PDF ([The Test Advantage](https://thetestadvantage.com/blog/is-desmos-allowed-on-the-sat)). The only 2026–27 change that touches math is that **students can now resize the calculator**. No content or scoring changes have been announced ([College Board changes](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/k12-educators/educator-experience/in-school/start/changes)). Practice should run on the College Board configuration at desmos.com/testing/collegeboard/graphing ([The Test Advantage](https://thetestadvantage.com/blog/how-to-use-desmos-on-the-sat-complete-may-2026-guide)). The tutor's headless Desmos instance should copy that configuration as closely as the API allows, so the AI never teaches a move that fails on test day.

Official practice material is scarce, and it is legally off-limits for the AI. Bluebook offers **8 full-length tests (Tests 4–11)**, and Test 11 (early 2026) is regarded as the closest to recent real tests ([The Test Advantage](https://thetestadvantage.com/blog/how-many-practice-tests-does-bluebook-have); [IvyTutors](https://ivytutorsnetwork.com/blog/march-2026-sat-changes-bluebook-test-11)). The free Educator Question Bank holds **about 1,900–2,000 math items**. Sigma Prep counted 1,876 in September 2026, including 196 added quietly in August ([Sigma Prep](https://www.sigmaprep.io/sat-question-bank); [Sigma Prep update](https://www.sigmaprep.io/blog/sat-question-bank-august-2026-new-questions)). The course can send students to these resources. It cannot ingest them (see the legal section). Sites that publish "question banks" reconstructed from live 2026 administrations ([example](https://thetestadvantage.com/test/dsat-math-october-2026-question-bank)) probably violate test security, and the course should neither use them nor link to them.

## Thirteen Desmos moves cover the solvable third to half of the test

Estimates of how much of the test Desmos can handle range from **about one-third** (Method Learning, the only estimate based on actually counting practice-test items) to **about half** (Miyagi Labs, PrepMaven), with an outlier marketing claim of 90% ([Method Learning](https://www.methodlearning.com/blog/the-power-of-desmos-on-the-digital-sat-math-section); [Miyagi Labs](https://miyagilabs.ai/blog/desmos-sat-tips-guide); [SATmaxxing](https://www.satmaxxing.com/desmos)). Most of the spread comes from definitions. "Desmos solves it outright" lands near 30–50%, while "Desmos helps somewhere" runs higher. The defensible marketing line is "Desmos solves or verifies roughly a third to half of questions." That framing also justifies the "Desmos first, by hand second" structure. Desmos is the primary method on a large, identifiable subset, and the safety net on much of the rest.

Prep sources agree closely on a core library of moves. The table below is the recommended spine of the curriculum and of the AI's retrieval library. Each move carries a by-hand counterpart. Rows marked "contested" are those where sources disagree about whether Desmos should come first.

| # | Desmos move | SAT question types | Canonical syntax / action | Known pitfalls | By-hand role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Graph both sides, read intersection or zeros | Any one-variable equation | `y=left`, `y=right`; or `left-right=0` and read x-intercepts | Off-screen intersections ("you didn't zoom") ([OnePrep](https://www.oneprep.co/blog/sat-math-tips-desmos-2026-12-calculator-moves)) | Skip Desmos if solvable in under ~15 s ([Miyagi Labs](https://miyagilabs.ai/blog/desmos-sat-tips-guide)) |
| T2 | Systems by intersection | Linear and nonlinear systems | Enter both, click the intersection ([The Test Advantage](https://thetestadvantage.com/blog/systems-of-equations-on-desmos-sat-math-shortcut-guide)) | Overlap means infinitely many solutions, parallel means none ([CheetahPrep](https://www.cheetahprep.com/desmos/system-linear-equations-infinite)) | Elimination for clean integers |
| T3 | Sliders for unknown constants (contested) | "For what k does the system have no / infinitely many / one solution" | Type `k`, accept the slider, drag until coincident, parallel or tangent ([The Test Advantage](https://thetestadvantage.com/blog/sat-infinite-solutions-systems-on-desmos-may-2026-full-guide)) | Say Hello College says avoid Desmos for parallel-line systems ([Say Hello College](https://sayhellocollege.com/blog/guide-to-using-the-desmos-sat-calculator/)) | **Primary:** coefficient ratios in Ax+By=C form, then graph to confirm |
| T4 | Sliders, general | Parameters, "how many solutions" | Undefined letters prompt sliders ([EdisonOS](https://www.edisonos.com/digital-sat/desmos-guide)) | Slider ranges may hide the answer | Discriminant reasoning |
| T5 | Table regression | Line or curve of best fit, model from points | `y_1 ~ m x_1 + b`; `y_1 ~ a b^{x_1}` ([EdisonOS](https://www.edisonos.com/digital-sat/desmos-guide)) | Needs at least 2 points. CB version forces Log Mode on exponential, log and power fits ([CB PDF](https://www.desmos.com/assessment-pdfs/CollegeBoard_Desmos_Calculator.pdf)) | Slope formula for 2 points |
| T6 | Tilde "find the constants" | Equivalent expressions with unknown a, b, c | `(5x_1−17)(4x_1−c) ~ a x_1^2 + b x_1 + 119` with `x_1=[2,3,4]` gives c=7, a=20, b=−103 ([Resolve Prep](https://resolveprep.com/blog/desmos-regression-sat-guide)) | Leaving out the x₁ list fails silently. Returns only one solution. Don't retype rounded decimals | Expand and match coefficients |
| T7 | Lists and statistics | Mean, median, SD, range | `mean([…])`, `median`, `stdev`, `stdevp`, `quartile` ([Desmos Supported Functions](https://help.desmos.com/hc/en-us/articles/212235786-Supported-Functions)) | `{}` is a restriction, not a list ([Learner Labs](https://learnerlabs.app/sat-prep/desmos/stats-from-list)) | Conceptual "what happens to the mean" items |
| T8 | Inequality shading | Systems of inequalities, "which point satisfies" | Type `<=`/`>=`. Dashed boundary means strict, solid means inclusive ([Effortless Math](https://www.effortlessmath.com/blog/desmos-sat-math-cheat-sheet-12-moves/)) | Overlapping colors mislead, so confirm with a test point | Substitute the answer choice |
| T9 | Define and evaluate functions | Composition, f(3) | `f(x)=2x+5`, then `f(3)` ([EdisonOS](https://www.edisonos.com/digital-sat/desmos-guide)) | Variable-name collisions | Direct substitution |
| T10 | Circles from expanded form (contested) | Center and radius | Graph the expanded form, then use the midpoint of the top and bottom points ([Pursu](https://pursu.io/guide/sat-circles-made-easy-7-ways-to-solve-geometry-problems-using-desmos-with-examples); [Learner Labs](https://learnerlabs.app/sat-prep/desmos/circles)) | Left and right points not reliably marked. Say Hello College says avoid | **Primary:** complete the square |
| T11 | Exact vs decimal | SPR entry, fraction answers | Fraction toggle (5.125 becomes 41/8). Store exact values in variables ([LearnQ](https://blogs.learnq.ai/math-calcutor-desmos-digital-sat/)) | Approximate values read as exact | Exact algebra for SPR ([Sherpalai](https://sherpalai.com/sat-r-desmos-calculator-tips/sat-math-desmos-cheat-sheet)) |
| T12 | Trig with angle mode | Trig evaluation | Check degrees vs radians first ([Acely](https://acely.com/sat-prep/desmos-cheat-sheet)) | The most costly trig error | Special right triangles, unit circle |
| T13 | Graph or plug answer choices | Multiple choice of any type | Enter each option and eliminate ([Strategic Test Prep](https://www.strategictestprep.com/post/6-desmos-hacks-to-boost-your-sat-math-score)) | Slow when all four must be tested | Estimation and elimination |

A few further moves are useful but **unsourced in the notes**, so test them in the College Board calculator before teaching them: clicking a parabola's gray points of interest to read the vertex, `sum`/`prod` and `[1...10]` list generation, absolute-value solution counting with a slider, `%` syntax, `{0<x<5}` domain restrictions, and how implicit multiplication parses (`ab` is read as a·b, so use `a_1`). The tilde trick (T6) and sliders (T3) separate basic from advanced Desmos instruction. T6 is also the most algorithmic move, which makes it the easiest for an AI to generate and verify automatically.

The Desmos-versus-by-hand boundary is the course's real intellectual property, and sources only sketch it. They agree that Desmos is weak or slower on **word-problem translation, geometry and trig reasoning, PSDA interpretation (study design, margin of error), definitions, and abstract "which must be true" structure questions**. Aniko recommends trying algebraic structure, geometry and special triangles before graphing ([Aniko](https://www.aniko.ai/free-resources/sat/courses/desmos/when-not-to-use-desmos)). Say Hello College advises skipping Desmos on the last four Module 2 problems unless it obviously applies ([Say Hello College](https://sayhellocollege.com/blog/guide-to-using-the-desmos-sat-calculator/)). Strategic Test Prep argues that College Board has added unknown-constant items that make Desmos slower ([Strategic Test Prep](https://www.strategictestprep.com/post/is-desmos-dead-on-the-sat-in-2026)). That claim is unverified and partly undercut by T3 and T6, which handle unknown constants directly. MathChops' tutor advice is the right governing rule: **use Desmos only when you know how you will use it, and be able to solve every type both ways** ([MathChops](https://mathchops.substack.com/p/a-five-minute-dsat-desmos-intro)). The course should therefore tag each College Board skill with a default route (Desmos-primary, hand-primary, or either), a 15-second hand-check threshold, and a mandatory pre-flight checklist before the student trusts a Desmos answer: angle mode, zoom to fit, parentheses, `[]` vs `{}`, and whether an exact form is required. No published per-skill timing study exists. **Building and publishing that taxonomy with timing data from the product's own users** is both a differentiator and a moat.

## The AI learns Desmos through a verified tool loop, not fine-tuning

### Model and feature choices

The current Gemini line is 3.x. **Gemini 3.1 Pro (preview) has a ~1M-token context window, function calling, code execution and structured output, at $2 input and $12 output per million tokens for prompts up to 200K tokens** ([Google Cloud docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/models/gemini/3-1-pro); [CloudZero](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/gemini-pricing/)). **Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite costs about $0.25/$1.50, and Batch runs at roughly 50% off** ([Puter](https://developer.puter.com/tutorials/gemini-api-pricing/)). Mid-tier Flash names and prices ("3.5 Flash", "3.6–3.8 Flash") conflict across sources and could not be confirmed ([benchlm](https://benchlm.ai/google/api-pricing)). Models turn over fast. Several 2.5 preview versions shut down within months, and stable 2.5 Flash and Flash-Lite on the Agent Platform (Vertex) route shut down **October 20, 2026** ([Gemini deprecations](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/deprecations); [Firebase models](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/models)). The recommended split puts **Pro on offline work** (generating solution traces, judging evals, escalating hard items) and **a Flash or Flash-Lite tier on live tutoring**. The long system prompt and technique library should go in context caching, and the model IDs should be pinned, with a model-swap regression run before any upgrade. The Live API (`gemini-3.1-flash-live-preview`, with screen-share and tool use) is a phase-2 option at most ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/build-with-gemini-3-1-flash-live/)). Voiceprints are now "personal information" under the amended COPPA Rule ([Cullen LLP](https://www.cullenllp.com/blog/ftc-publishes-coppa-rule-amendments/)), so voice features add compliance cost.

Bulk offline work is cheap. As a rough estimate from the Pro list price above, 1,000 solver runs at 10K input and 3K output tokens each come to about $20 + $36 = $56, or about $28 on Batch. The constraints that matter are legal and engineering ones, not inference spend.

Fine-tuning is effectively unavailable. **The Gemini Developer API has had no tuning since Gemini 1.5 Flash-001 was deprecated in May 2025** ([Google AI forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/gemini-api-fine-tuning-disabled/92763)). Vertex supervised fine-tuning covers only 2.5-series models, and **no Gemini 3.x model supports SFT**, with no timeline announced ([aifreeapi](https://www.aifreeapi.com/en/posts/how-to-fine-tune-gemini-custom-model); [Google Dev forum](https://discuss.google.dev/t/when-will-supervised-fine-tuning-be-available-for-gemini-3-x-models-on-vertex-ai/346487)). Tuning a model the platform retires this month would be wasted work. The plan should treat "training" as prompt, retrieval, tool and eval engineering. Every verified trace should still be saved in a provider-neutral format so it can become an SFT corpus later, whether 3.x tuning opens up or the team moves to an open model.

### The headless Desmos engine

The Desmos API (v1.12) provides the primitives a verification tool needs. `HelperExpression` exposes observable `numericValue` and `listValue` for any expression. `expressionAnalysis` reports, for each expression, whether it errored and why. `asyncScreenshot` waits for full evaluation before returning a PNG or SVG ([Desmos API](https://www.desmos.com/api); [v1.0 docs](https://www.desmos.com/api/v1.0/docs/index.html); [v1.11 docs](https://www.desmos.com/api/v1.11/docs/index.html)). After a regression runs, its fitted parameters are stored as variables ([Desmos Help: Regressions](https://help.desmos.com/hc/en-us/articles/4406972958733-Regressions)). There is precedent for running it headless. **MCP-Desmos (MIT) already drives the official API in headless Chromium through Playwright for LLMs** ([GitHub](https://github.com/AndresMuelas2004/MCP-Desmos)). The recommended tool is a Node/Playwright service exposing `desmos_run({expressions, queries})`. It sets expressions, waits for evaluation to settle, and returns structured JSON with per-expression errors, helper values and an optional screenshot. Errors are returned as data so the model can correct itself.

Two API limitations shape the design. First, **no documented API route was found for the gray "points of interest"** (intersections, extrema) that students click, and none for R². Every trace therefore needs two layers. One is a student-facing keystroke script ("graph both, click the intersection"). The other is a machine-checkable query that independently confirms the value, such as substituting the candidate into a HelperExpression. Second, Desmos is the teaching medium but should not be the only oracle. Each answer should also be checked by SymPy or code execution, so a Desmos quirk can never certify a wrong answer.

### "Training" without weight updates

The research basis is tool-integrated reasoning. **PAL** has the model write programs that an interpreter executes. **ToRA** (ICLR 2024) interleaves reasoning with tool calls and improves by keeping only valid sampled trajectories, which it calls "output space shaping." **ToRL** and **CoRT** add RL and rejection sampling, and CoRT reports +4% to +8% across five math datasets ([ToRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.17452); [ToRL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.23383); [CoRT](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20342v1)). **No published work uses Desmos or GeoGebra as an LLM reasoning tool**, so the team will be measuring new territory. Applied without weight updates, the approach has six parts.

**A two-stage agent.** A Solver solves privately with Desmos calls until the Desmos result agrees with an independent SymPy computation. Only then does a Tutor receive the verified solution and Desmos steps as hidden context and run a Socratic dialogue. This separation answers MathTutorBench's central finding: "specializing an LLM for pedagogy comes at the cost of solving ability", and the reverse also holds ([MathTutorBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.18940v2)).

**A cached technique library with retrieval.** Expand the 13 moves to about 30–60 entries, each holding canonical LaTeX, trigger conditions, pitfalls and the by-hand fallback. Retrieve the top entries by item type, and keep 3–8 worked traces in the cached prompt.

**A defensive LaTeX normalizer.** It catches likely LLM errors before they reach Desmos: unbraced `\frac` or `x^10`, bare `sqrt`, mismatched `\left(` and `\right)`, `=` where `~` is required, multi-letter variables, stray markdown `$`, and degrees vs radians. This list is inferred, not measured. Feed `expressionAnalysis` error messages back for N retries, and log every error class to build the real taxonomy.

**Self-verification gates.** Substitute the answer back in, confirm it matches exactly one multiple-choice option, and run the SPR formatter. Any disagreement escalates to Pro.

**Rejection-sampled verified traces.** For each original item with a known key, sample K Pro trajectories and execute them headless. Keep only those with a correct answer and zero tool errors, preferring the shortest one a student could reproduce in Bluebook. The survivors become few-shot examples, retrieval entries and a future SFT corpus.

**Vision for student screenshots.** Gemini can read a photo of a student's Desmos screen, but the tutor should re-run the extracted expressions in the tool rather than trust the pixels.

### Evals that gate launch

The harness should score three layers separately. **Solver correctness** is pass@1 and self-consistency on a held-out set. **Tool hygiene** covers expression error rate per call, retries per item, the share of answers verified by the tool, latency and cost. **Tutoring quality** is measured through simulated-student dialogues with scripted misconceptions, scored for answer-reveal rate (which should be about zero before the student attempts), mistake identification and location, whether a student could reproduce each Desmos instruction in Bluebook, and tone. LLM judges are noisy. **MathTutorBench found judge accuracy below 0.7 and sensitivity to prompt and position** ([arXiv 2502.18940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18940)). The judge should therefore come from a different model family or Pro, use randomized pairwise order, and be calibrated against about 100 human-labeled dialogues before anyone trusts its scores. The benchmark should hold **300–500 original items stratified by domain, difficulty, multiple choice vs SPR, and Desmos-advantaged vs Desmos-neutral**, each with a reference Desmos solution verified headless. It must contain **no College Board items** (see the legal section). The full suite should run on every prompt, model or tool change, at Batch pricing. No public SAT-Desmos benchmark exists, and no data on Gemini 3.x accuracy on SAT math was found. Publishing a head-to-head accuracy benchmark on original hard items would double as the product's strongest marketing.

## No competitor's AI drives Desmos, while generic AI SAT help has gone free

Free AI SAT prep now comes from three large players. **Khanmigo is built into Khan Academy's official SAT prep at about $4/month, or free through districts** ([Khan blog](https://blog.khanacademy.org/sat-prep-ai-districts/); [devtoollab](https://devtoollab.com/ai-tools/khanmigo)). **Google put free full-length SAT practice tests built with The Princeton Review into Gemini in January 2026** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/22/google-now-offers-free-sat-practice-exams-powered-by-gemini)). **The Princeton Review launched a free SAT app inside ChatGPT in June 2026** ([Princeton Review](https://www.princetonreview.com/press/princeton-review-chatgpt-sat-app)). Around them sits a crowded field of AI-first startups (Acely, LearnQ, AlphaTest, Archer, DoublePrep, Mentis, Miyagi Labs, Crackd, Aniko) that all claim roughly the same mix of adaptive diagnostic, Socratic chat and Desmos tips ([Acely](https://acely.com/ai-sat-tutor); [Crackd](https://crackd.it/)). Incumbent pricing runs from UWorld at about $59–$449 to Princeton Review at $299–$2,199 and Kaplan at $199–$2,999 ([PrepGenix](https://prepgenix.com/blog/best-sat-prep-platforms-compared/); [educationdata.org](https://educationdata.org/best-sat-prep-courses); [The College Investor](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/45307/best-sat-test-prep/)). Desmos-specific courses cluster at **$0–$25/month**: SATDesmos at $25/month, 1600.io at $19.99/month or $99.99/year, PrepPros at $19.99/month, with free courses from LearnDesmos, Aniko and Cheetah Prep ([SATDesmos](https://www.satdesmos.com/pages/home); [1600.io](https://1600.io/p/all-access-basic-monthly-membership); [PrepPros](https://www.preppros.io/sat-math-course); [LearnDesmos](https://learndesmos.com/faq)). Desmos tips by themselves are commoditized and are used across the industry as SEO lead magnets.

The opening is accuracy and actual operation of Desmos at the top of the score range. **No product was found whose AI plots, moves sliders or reads intersections inside the tutoring conversation.** Aniko and Crackd embed a calculator that the student operates. Satified says outright that its AI "does not operate Desmos for the student" ([Satified](https://satified.org/blog/desmos-plus-ai-sat-math/); [Aniko](https://www.aniko.ai/blog/how-to-learn-desmos-for-the-sat)). Accuracy is the known weakness of AI prep. Reviewers found off-syllabus, unsolvable and multi-answer items in Gemini's own SAT tests ([MentoMind](https://mentomind.ai/gemini-sat-prep-review/); [CollegeTestCoach](https://www.collegetestcoach.com/blog/gemini-sat-prep-review)). In a high-school study, GPT-4's step-by-step approach **was wrong 42% of the time** ([Hechinger Report](https://hechingerreport.org/kids-chatgpt-worse-on-tests/)). High scorers also have specific complaints. Khan is "much less helpful for advanced students (scoring above 700)" ([PrepMaven](https://prepmaven.com/blog/test-prep/khan-academy-sat-prep-review/)), official tests run out, and unofficial items are badly calibrated ([ArborBridge](https://blog.arborbridge.com/digital-sat-six-ways-to-prep)).

The differentiated product combines four things: an AI that proves its answers by computation in a real Desmos, a published Desmos-or-hand decision trainer with timing, a validated bank of hard Module 2-style items, and a focus on the 700–800 band. A price of about $15–25/month or a $79–149 test-cycle pass would sit under incumbent pricing, though that range is inferred and untested. **The risks are real.** Google, Khan or College Board could ship AI that operates Desmos quickly. The white space is "absence of evidence," not proof. No direct complaints about AI getting Desmos wrong were found, so that pain point is inferred. And the SAT audience of **just over 2 million takers a year** ([College Board newsroom](https://newsroom.collegeboard.org/sat-participation-class-2025-surpasses-2-million-test-takers-first-time-2020)) narrows a lot at a 700+ math target. Customer interviews about willingness to pay should come before heavy build.

## Code grades, the LLM explains: a provider-agnostic stack

Because of the vendor uncertainty, the most important architecture decision is that **the LLM is a replaceable component behind the team's own orchestration layer**. The recommended stack, which is an architecture inference rather than a sourced finding, is a **Next.js/React/TypeScript frontend** that embeds the student-facing Desmos calculator through `Desmos.GraphingCalculator` with `getState`/`setState`/`setExpression`, so the tutor can read the student's graph and add scaffolds. Math renders in KaTeX, and responses stream over SSE through a multi-provider SDK such as the Vercel AI SDK. A **server-only orchestration service** (Next.js route handlers or Cloud Run) holds the keys, the system prompt, the tool definitions (`check_answer`, `get_hint(level)`, `set_desmos_expression`, `log_misconception`), safety filters and per-user quotas. Two services sit beside it: the **headless Desmos verification service** and a **SymPy verification service**. Progress data lives in **Supabase (Postgres, Auth, row-level security)** or the Firebase equivalent, and **Stripe** handles parent-paid billing.

The governing rule is that **canonical answers live in the database and a deterministic grader marks every attempt. The LLM never decides correctness or mastery.** An Elo/IRT engine chooses the next item. Firebase AI Logic is the quickest route to Gemini, but it ties the product to Gemini and enforces a **default 100 requests per minute per user, applied project-wide**, with no per-user overrides ([Firebase AI Logic quotas](https://firebase.google.com/docs/ai-logic/quotas?api=vertex)). Desmos Starter is **hosted by Desmos only**, so the calculator loads from Desmos's CDN with an API key. Self-hosting requires Enterprise ([Desmos my-api](https://www.desmos.com/my-api)). Cost control comes from caching per-item explanations and the shared prompt, capping tokens per turn, setting daily message quotas, and sending easy turns to the smallest model.

## Gemini's under-18 clause, College Board's AI ban and Desmos licensing all block launch as currently scoped

**Gemini.** The Gemini API Additional Terms say "You must be 18 years of age or older to use the APIs." They also prohibit an "API Client directed towards or likely to be accessed by individuals under the age of 18" ([Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). **Google Cloud Service Specific Terms §17(d) apply the same restriction to generative AI on the Agent Platform (formerly Vertex AI)** ([Google Cloud terms](https://cloud.google.com/terms/service-terms)). A forum reply says the restriction applies however the API is accessed, and Google declined to give legal guidance on routing minors elsewhere ([Google Dev forum](https://discuss.google.dev/t/question-regarding-age-restrictions-for-the-gemini-api/188060/2); [AI Dev forum](https://discuss.ai.google.dev/t/age-restriction-under-18-is-routing-minors-away-from-gemini-api-acceptable/188423)). At least one project treated this as a hard blocker and changed LLM vendor ([GitHub issue](https://github.com/AI-X-16-1/BOOKIT/issues/54)). Google does serve teens Gemini through Workspace for Education and its own consumer SAT practice. Those are first-party products, not a developer carve-out ([Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2024/10/gemini-app-access-for-teen-students-workspace-education.html)). There are three options. The team can get a **written exception or enterprise agreement from a Google Cloud account team**. It can **move the tutor to another provider whose terms permit 13–17 users**, though those terms were not researched and must be checked. Or it can **restrict to verified 18+ users**, which misses most of the market and may still fail the "likely to be accessed by" test. Whichever path is taken, use **Paid Services only**. On the free tier, human reviewers may read prompts and outputs and Google may use them to improve products, which is incompatible with handling minors' data ([Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)).

**College Board content.** College Board says official SAT materials may not be reproduced in test-prep or commercial settings, and that it **does not grant permission for its content, including practice questions, to be used with generative AI** ([CB permissions](https://privacy.collegeboard.org/copyright-trademark/request-instructions); [CB guidelines](https://privacy.collegeboard.org/copyright-trademark/guidelines)). This rules out official items in prompts, few-shot examples, retrieval, eval sets and trace generation, even though Bluebook and Question Bank items would make the most realistic benchmark. The course may align to the published domains and skill descriptions. It should write its own items, keep provenance logs, and link out to Bluebook and the Question Bank. Using "SAT" descriptively requires the **non-affiliation footnote on every page that uses the mark** ([CB trademark guidelines](https://privacy.collegeboard.org/copyright-trademark/guidelines)). Khan Academy material is CC BY-NC-SA, so link to it but do not embed it in a paid product ([Khan Help](https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/202262954-Can-I-use-Khan-Academy-s-videos-name-materials-links-)).

**Original item generation.** This is a pipeline in its own right. Cross-model verification flagged **63.6% of flawed MCQs versus 27.3% for self-verification** ([IEEE Access 2025](https://profile.cpce-polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/automatic-generation-of-high-quality-mcqs-with-llms-for-artificia-2/)), and whether a question is well-posed is a separate failure mode from a wrong key ([ValiMath](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.13903v2)). Items should come from parameterized templates where code computes the key. They then pass a deterministic SymPy check, a blind solve by a second model, a near-duplicate check against public items, and human review, followed by IRT recalibration once real responses arrive.

**Desmos licensing.** The free tier covers only personal non-commercial use or a **90-day internal evaluation**. "Commercial use includes any use of the API in an Application that is accessed by End Users in production" ([Desmos API terms](https://www.desmos.com/api-terms)). **Starter starts at about $100/month for up to 1,000 users** (Desmos's pages are summarized as $100–$300 across 1,000/2,500/5,000-user tiers). It is Desmos-hosted only. **Enterprise is custom-priced and adds self-hosting** ([Desmos Help](https://help.desmos.com/hc/en-us/articles/49078363315725); [Desmos my-api](https://www.desmos.com/my-api)). It is unclear whether a server-side headless instance used only for AI verification, which no end user ever sees, is covered by Starter. The team should email partnerships@desmos.com before building on that assumption.

**Minors' privacy.** The amended COPPA Rule required compliance by **April 22, 2026**, adds biometrics to personal information, and requires naming third-party recipients ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule); [BBB Programs](https://bbbprograms.org/media/insights/blog/coppa-amended)). COPPA covers under-13 users. A neutral date-of-birth screen with verifiable parental consent or blocking for under-13s is still needed, because some younger students will arrive. California requires **opt-in for selling or sharing data of under-16s** once CCPA thresholds apply ([CA AB 801 analysis](https://apcp.assembly.ca.gov/sites/privacycp.assembly.ca.gov/files/AB%20801%20(Joe%20Patterson)%20APCP%20analysis.pdf)). A school sales channel adds FERPA "school official" duties and SOPIPA's bans on targeted advertising and profiling ([FPF SOPIPA guide](https://fpf.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/11/SOPIPA-Guide_Nov-4-2016.pdf); [Edlink](https://ed.link/community/student-privacy-laws-edtech-developers-need-to-know/)). The notes did not cover state age-appropriate design codes, Utah and Texas age-verification laws, or state chatbot-for-minors laws, all of which may apply to a teen AI chat product in 2026. Counsel must cover them.

## Guardrails decide whether an AI tutor raises or lowers test scores

The strongest evidence is a warning. In **Bastani et al. (PNAS, 2025), a randomized trial with about 1,000 high-school math students**, unguarded "GPT Base" raised practice performance by 48% but **cut unassisted exam scores by 17%**, and its users believed they had learned as much as their peers. A hint-based "GPT Tutor" with guardrails raised practice performance 127% with **no exam penalty** ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486); [Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education)). Well-designed AI tutoring can work. A carefully prompted AI tutor roughly **doubled learning gains over in-class active learning in less time** in a Harvard physics crossover study (N=194) ([Scientific Reports via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/)), and a World Bank RCT in Nigeria found **+0.31 SD** ([World Bank WP 11125](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/11125.html)). Engagement is the constraint at scale. In an NBER two-year study of Khanmigo, the median student messaged on only about a third of practice days ([NBER w35620](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35620)). Spacing and interleaving reliably improve math test scores, yet only 9.7% of textbook problems are interleaved ([Rohrer et al. 2020](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-020-09516-2)).

This has a direct consequence for a Desmos-first course. **Showing the AI operating Desmos is the crutch risk in its purest form.** If the tutor graphs and the student watches, the result is GPT Base. The tutor's Desmos ability should stay mostly hidden. It verifies answers, diagnoses the student's own Desmos state, and **makes the student reproduce the keystrokes in an embedded College Board-configured calculator**. Hints come in stages (nudge, strategy, worked step, full solution) and only after a committed attempt. A near-transfer item follows every revealed solution. Each Desmos solution is paired with a quick by-hand estimate, and an occasional calculator-off check item measures over-reliance. The session loop is a spaced warm-up drawn from the error log, a worked example followed by faded examples for the new move, interleaved mixed practice, and timed 22-question mini-modules at about 1.5 minutes per item. Per-skill Elo or IRT targets a 70–85% success rate. Misconception tags come from a fixed taxonomy and resurface on an FSRS-style schedule. Parent progress emails, streaks and short sessions address engagement within the privacy rules. No SAT-specific RCT of AI tutoring and no study of Desmos over-reliance were found, so the product should run its own A/B evaluation of guardrail settings.

## Conclusion

The defensible version of this product is not the one in the brief, "Gemini tutor teaches Desmos." It is a **verification company that happens to teach**: an item bank and technique taxonomy whose every answer has been executed in real Desmos and cross-checked in code, fronted by whichever LLM the law allows. Everything that makes the AI "good at Desmos" (the headless engine, the normalizer, the verified traces, the eval suite) is owned by the team and portable across vendors. That matters because the named vendor is the single largest risk. The cheapest high-value move this month is a Phase 0 sprint of legal letters, a Desmos licensing email and a hands-on Bluebook audit, before any engineering beyond the trial-key prototype.

Two less obvious implications follow. First, the AI's Desmos capability should be mostly invisible to students. The Bastani evidence implies that its value lies in guaranteeing correctness and diagnosing the student's own keystrokes, not in performing for them. Second, the most valuable public artifacts are the ones no competitor has: a per-skill "Desmos or by hand" timing map built from real student data, and an open accuracy benchmark of AI tutors on original hard items. Both turn the product's internal quality machinery into marketing that free incumbents cannot easily copy.
