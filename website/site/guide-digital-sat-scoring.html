How the digital SAT is scored

The SAT doesn't count your right answers and look them up on a chart. It's adaptive, and which questions you get right matters as well as how many. Here's what that means for how you practice.

On this page

  1. The shape of the test
  2. Module 1 picks Module 2
  3. Why there's no official chart
  4. Reading a practice score
  5. What to do about it

The shape of the test

The digital SAT has two sections, taken in Bluebook, College Board's testing app:

  • Reading and Writing: 54 questions in 64 minutes, as two 32-minute modules of 27 questions.
  • Math: 44 questions in 70 minutes, as two 35-minute modules of 22 questions. The Desmos graphing calculator is built in for the whole section.

Each section is scored from 200 to 800, so the total runs from 400 to 1600. A few questions in each module are unscored pretest questions mixed in with the rest. You can't tell which ones, so treat every question as if it counts.

Module 1 picks Module 2

Each section is a two-stage adaptive test. Everyone gets a Module 1 with a broad mix of easy, medium and hard questions. How you do on it decides whether your Module 2 is the harder or the easier version.

  • The harder Module 2 has more hard questions and is the only route to the top of the scale.
  • The easier Module 2 caps the section score. College Board doesn't publish the cap; third-party estimates put it somewhere around 560 to 650.

That's why Module 1 accuracy matters so much. A careless miss on an easy Module 1 question can cost you twice: once for the question, and again if it tips you onto the easier route.

Why there's no official chart

The SAT is scored with item response theory. Each question has its own measured difficulty, and your score comes from the pattern of questions you got right, not only the count. Two students with the same number right can get different scores.

So College Board publishes no raw-to-scaled conversion table for the digital SAT. Every "how many can I miss" chart online, including ours, is an estimate built from Bluebook practice test results. Treat the numbers as ranges. For a sense of scale, third-party estimates for Math on the harder route put 41 of 44 right at around 740 and 35 of 44 at around 640.

Reading a practice score

Bluebook's full-length practice tests are the closest thing to the real test, and their scores are the best predictor you have. Any other practice score, ours included, is an estimate. Read it like this:

  • As a range, not a number. One test is a sample. A score of 1300 really means somewhere around 1250 to 1350, and a jump of 30 points between two tests is often noise.
  • Shorter tests mean wider ranges. A single module or a 20-question check tells you less than a full section, so its range should be wider. Graphite's results show the range for exactly that reason.
  • Trends beat single scores. Three practice tests moving the same way tell you more than one good day.

What to do about it

  • Protect Module 1. Slow down enough on easy and medium questions to get them all. They keep you on the harder route.
  • Don't skip hard questions on principle. Hard questions you get right are what open the top of the scale. Make a quick attempt, mark it, and come back.
  • Answer everything. There's no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is always worse than a guess.
  • Take full tests in Bluebook every few weeks, under real timing, and practice between them on the skills those tests show are costing you.
