How Reading and Writing works

The Reading and Writing section is two modules of 27 questions, with 32 minutes for each. Every question comes with its own short text, from a sentence or two up to about 150 words, and four answer choices. You never have to hold a long passage in your head.

The second module adapts. Do well on the first and the second one is harder, which is the only way to reach the top scores. So accuracy on module 1 matters more than speed.

Questions come in a fixed order, grouped by type: Craft and Structure (vocabulary, purpose, paired texts), then Information and Ideas (main idea, evidence, inferences), then Standard English Conventions (grammar and punctuation), then Expression of Ideas (transitions and notes). Within each type, questions get harder as you go.

How to do it

  1. Read the question first. It tells you what to look for: a word's meaning, the main idea, a piece of evidence, a punctuation mark.
  2. Read the whole text. It's short, and the clue you need is often in the sentence you would have skipped.
  3. Predict before you look. Say the answer in your own words, even roughly (“something like ‘uncertain’”). A prediction protects you from choices that sound good.
  4. Eliminate. Each wrong choice has a specific flaw: too strong, off topic, reversed, only half right. Find it, then cross the choice out.
  5. Pick the choice the text proves. If you'd need to add an assumption or outside knowledge to make a choice work, it's wrong.

Traps to avoid

True, but not the answer
The choice is accurate to the text or the world, but it doesn't answer the question that was asked.
Too strong
Words like proves, always, only and entirely go further than most texts do.
Half right
The first half fits; the second half adds something the text never says. Read every choice to the last word.
Outside knowledge
The answer comes only from the text, even when you know more about the topic.

Pacing

Question typeTarget time
Grammar and punctuation (Boundaries; Form, structure, and sense)30–45 seconds
Transitions and Rhetorical synthesisAbout 45 seconds
Reading questions (everything else)About 75 seconds
Whole moduleAbout 70 seconds a question on average

Quick grammar questions buy time for harder reading questions. If you're past two minutes on one question, pick your best remaining choice, mark it for review and move on. There's no penalty for a wrong answer, so never leave one blank.

Craft and Structure · 8 min

Words in context

These questions give you a short text with a blank or an underlined word. You choose the word that fits most logically and precisely, or the meaning the word has in this text.

The words are ones you'll meet in college reading, but the question never rewards knowing a dictionary definition by heart. It rewards reading the clues around the blank.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?
  • As used in the text, what does the word “______” most nearly mean?

How to do it

  1. Cover the choices. Read the whole text, not only the sentence with the blank.
  2. Find the clue. Look for a contrast (but, although, yet), a restatement after a colon, or a cause and effect. The text almost always explains the missing word somewhere else.
  3. Fill the blank yourself. A rough word is fine: “good”, “not sure yet”, “mixed”.
  4. Match. Find the choice closest to your word. Check that it has the right charge (positive or negative) and the right strength.
  5. Reread the sentence with your choice in it. It should say exactly what the rest of the text says.

Traps to avoid

The common meaning
In meaning questions, one choice is the word's everyday sense (a pointed stick). The text usually uses a less common sense.
Right topic, wrong direction
The word fits the subject but reverses the logic, for example a positive word where the clue says the reaction was doubtful.
Too strong or too weak
A text that says results suggest something won't support proved or confirmed.
Hard-word bias
Don't pick a word just because you don't know it. Eliminate the words you do know first. If they all fail, the unknown word is the answer.

Worked example

Example
Early reviews of the bridge design were ______: engineers praised its elegant arch but questioned whether its thin deck could carry heavy trucks. The city asked for more tests before approving it.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

  1. A

    A. enthusiastic

    This ignores the doubts about the deck. Engineers weren't simply excited.

  2. B

    B. mixed Answer

    Correct. The colon introduces the explanation: praise for the arch plus doubts about the deck. That's a mixed reaction, and it explains why the city wanted more tests.

  3. C

    C. hostile

    This ignores the praise. The engineers liked the arch.

  4. D

    D. delayed

    The text is about what the reviews said, not when they arrived.

How to think about it

The colon is the clue: what follows it explains the blank. Predict “some good, some bad” before you look at the choices.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Botanist Lena Ortiz cautions that her results are ______: the drought-tolerant wheat grew well in her greenhouse trials, but it hasn't yet been tested in open fields, where weather and pests vary.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or phrase?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. Ortiz cautions, and the text explains why: the wheat worked in the greenhouse but hasn't faced real fields yet. Her results are early, or preliminary. Conclusive is the opposite. Nothing says the results don't matter (irrelevant) or that people are arguing about them (controversial).

Quick check 2 of 2
In the novel, the old fisherman rarely speaks, but when he does, his remarks are so pointed that the younger crew members fall silent and reconsider their plans.

As used in the text, what does the word “pointed” most nearly mean?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. His remarks make the crew stop and rethink their plans, so they hit the mark: they're direct and meaningful. Sharp-tipped is the everyday meaning for objects. Nothing says he's rude. And he rarely speaks, so his remarks aren't frequent.

Craft and Structure · 8 min

Text structure and purpose

These questions ask about the text as a piece of writing, not just what it says. Why did the author write it? How is it organized? What job does one sentence do?

The answer choices almost always start with a verb: to describe, to argue, to challenge, to illustrate. Getting the verb right is half the question.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?
  • Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?
  • Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

How to do it

  1. Label each sentence in a few words as you read: “old belief”, “new claim”, “reason”, “example”, “result”.
  2. For purpose, ask why the author wrote this. Is it to explain something, argue for it, describe it, or correct a belief?
  3. For one sentence's function, look at the sentences right before and after it. Does it give an example, a reason, a contrast or a conclusion?
  4. For structure, string your labels together (“belief, then evidence against it”) and find the choice with the same shape.
  5. Check the verb. A choice with the right topic and the wrong verb is wrong.

Traps to avoid

Right topic, wrong verb
The text describes a finding, but the choice says it argues or criticizes.
A detail posing as the purpose
The choice describes one sentence, not the text as a whole.
The wrong tone
A neutral, informative text won't mock or dismiss anyone.

Worked example

Example
For decades, people assumed that sloths move slowly because they are lazy. Biologist Rafael Ochoa argues that the slowness is a strategy. Sloths eat leaves that provide very little energy, and by moving slowly and keeping a low body temperature, they use less energy than they take in. Ochoa notes that a sloth may spend a week digesting a single meal.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  1. A

    A. It gives an example of how slowly a sloth's body works, illustrating the low-energy way of life Ochoa describes. Answer

    Correct. The sentence before it says sloths save energy by living slowly; a week-long digestion is a vivid example of that.

  2. B

    B. It introduces the assumption that Ochoa disputes.

    The disputed assumption (that sloths are lazy) is in the first sentence, not the underlined one.

  3. C

    C. It questions whether Ochoa's evidence is reliable.

    Nothing in the sentence casts doubt on Ochoa; it comes from Ochoa.

  4. D

    D. It shows that the sloth's diet provides more energy than Ochoa claims.

    This reverses the text, which says the leaves provide very little energy.

How to think about it

Labels: old belief → Ochoa's claim → reason → underlined sentence. The underlined sentence adds a specific case, so its job is to illustrate.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Many people think of glaciers as frozen and still. In fact, glaciers flow. Under their own enormous weight, the ice near the bottom slowly deforms and slides over the rock below, and the fastest glaciers in Greenland move more than 30 meters a day.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. The text states a common belief (“frozen and still”), says it's wrong (“In fact, glaciers flow”), and explains how they move. That's correcting a belief. The text never mentions melting, other places or danger.

Quick check 2 of 2
The following text is from a short story.

Nadia had practiced the speech for weeks, and in her bedroom every word came easily. But when she stepped onto the auditorium stage and saw the rows of faces, the first line vanished. She looked down at her note cards, found her place, and began, slowly at first and then with a steadiness that surprised her.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Easy practice (confidence), the forgotten first line (setback), then a steady finish (recovery). Only Nadia appears, so there's no contrast between characters. The text never says she dislikes speaking. And she does solve the problem.

Craft and Structure · 8 min

Cross-text connections

You get two short texts on the same topic, labeled Text 1 and Text 2. The question asks how they relate, usually how the author of Text 2 would respond to something in Text 1.

This is usually the hardest question in Craft and Structure, because you have to keep two views apart and pin down exactly where they meet.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to the “conventional wisdom” discussed in Text 1?
  • Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?

How to do it

  1. Sum up Text 1 in a few words: whose view is it, and what is the claim?
  2. Sum up Text 2 the same way. Then note how it relates: does it agree, disagree, or agree with a condition (“yes, but only partly”)?
  3. Find the exact target. The question often points to one claim, one group or one method in Text 1, not the whole text.
  4. Predict how Text 2's author would react to that target, then match.

Traps to avoid

Mixing the voices
An author often reports a view in order to argue with it. Don't treat a reported view as the author's own.
Too extreme
Text 2 says the claim is incomplete; the choice says it's completely wrong. Partial disagreement is the most common relationship.
The wrong direction
The question asks how Text 2 would respond to Text 1; a choice describes how Text 1 would respond to Text 2.

Worked example

Example
Text 1
Many researchers have concluded that city birds sing at a higher pitch because traffic noise is low-pitched, and higher songs are easier for other birds to hear over it.

Text 2
Ecologist Ji-woo Han agrees that city birds sing higher but doubts that noise alone explains it. In her recordings, birds in quiet city parks also sang higher than forest birds, which suggests that city surfaces, such as hard walls that echo sound, may also play a role.

Based on the texts, how would Han most likely respond to the researchers' conclusion in Text 1?

  1. A

    A. She would argue that city birds don't actually sing at a higher pitch.

    Han agrees that city birds sing higher. She questions the cause, not the fact.

  2. B

    B. She would say that noise may be part of the explanation, but not all of it. Answer

    Correct. Han “doubts that noise alone explains it” and suggests another factor that “may also play a role”. That's partial agreement.

  3. C

    C. She would agree that traffic noise is the only cause of the change.

    This is what Han doubts.

  4. D

    D. She would argue that forest birds sing at a higher pitch than city birds.

    This reverses her data: city birds sang higher than forest birds.

How to think about it

Text 1: noise causes higher songs. Text 2: higher songs, yes; noise, only partly. Predict “partly right” and look for that.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Text 1
Many nutrition guides advise eating breakfast every day, warning that people who skip it overeat later and gain weight.

Text 2
In a twelve-week trial led by researcher Tomás Rivera, adults were randomly assigned either to eat breakfast or to skip it. The two groups ended the trial with nearly identical changes in weight, though the breakfast group reported feeling less hungry before lunch.

Based on the texts, how would Rivera most likely respond to the warning in Text 1?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Weight changed about the same in both groups, so the trial doesn't back the warning. The second choice goes too far: skippers didn't lose more weight. The third contradicts the hunger finding. The fourth contradicts the weight result.

Quick check 2 of 2
Text 1
Architect Selin Aydın argues that new libraries should devote less space to bookshelves and more to rooms where people can meet, study together and use computers, because that is how visitors increasingly use libraries.

Text 2
Librarian Marcus Webb agrees that libraries must adapt to how visitors use them, but he warns that cutting shelf space too far drives away people who come to browse, a group his surveys show is still large.

Based on the texts, both authors would most likely agree with which statement?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Aydın bases her plan on how visitors use libraries, and Webb “agrees that libraries must adapt to how visitors use them”. They disagree about how far to cut shelves, so the second choice is only Aydın's view. Webb says browsers are still a large group, and neither author wants to remove computers.

Information and Ideas · 7 min

Central ideas and details

These questions ask what a text is mainly about, or what it says about a specific person or thing. In literary texts, they often ask what a character thinks, feels or wants.

The right answer is usually a close paraphrase. You aren't reading between the lines here; that's what Inferences questions are for.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice best states the main idea of the text?
  • According to the text, what is true about ______?
  • Based on the text, what is the narrator's main concern?

How to do it

  1. Read to the end. The main idea is often in the last sentence, or right after a turn word like however, instead or in fact.
  2. Say it in one sentence: who did or thinks what, and what they found.
  3. Check coverage. The right choice covers the whole text, not just one sentence of it.
  4. For a detail question, find the line that answers it, then pick the choice that restates that line.

Traps to avoid

Too narrow
One accurate detail, offered as if it were the whole point.
Too broad
A sweeping claim about a whole field when the text is about one study.
The setup, not the point
Many texts open with an expectation or old belief and then overturn it. A choice that states the expectation is reversed.
Borrowed words
The choice copies words from the text but changes what they say.

Worked example

Example
When the city of Linden painted some of its crosswalks with bright, colorful patterns, officials hoped drivers would slow down. Traffic engineer Priya Nair measured driver speeds before and after. Average speeds near the painted crosswalks dropped only slightly, but the number of drivers who stopped for waiting pedestrians nearly doubled.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  1. A

    A. Colorful crosswalks caused drivers in Linden to slow down sharply.

    This was the officials' hope, but speeds dropped “only slightly”.

  2. B

    B. Nair found that the painted crosswalks did little to lower speeds but made drivers much more likely to stop for pedestrians. Answer

    Correct. It covers both of Nair's findings, including the turn after “but”.

  3. C

    C. Linden painted its crosswalks mainly to make the city more attractive.

    The text says officials hoped to slow drivers. It says nothing about how the city looks.

  4. D

    D. Drivers in Linden often ignore traffic laws.

    Too broad, and the text doesn't support it.

How to think about it

The text opens with a hope and ends with what actually happened. The main idea is what happened.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
The following text is from a short story.

Every morning before school, Tomas checked the tomato plants on the balcony, counting the green fruit as if some of it might disappear overnight. His grandmother laughed at him, but she had started leaving the watering can by the door, where he would be sure to see it.

Based on the text, what is true about Tomas's grandmother?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. She laughs, “but” she leaves the watering can where Tomas will see it: a quiet way of supporting him. Nothing says she thinks the plants need no care or worries about theft (that's closer to Tomas's habit). The text says Tomas checks the plants; it never says she waters them.

Quick check 2 of 2
Bats are often thought to rely almost entirely on echolocation to find their way in the dark. Biologist Ana Ferreira trained fruit bats to fly through a maze in dim light and found that they steered by sight as well as by sound, switching mostly to echolocation only when the light became very low.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. The text sets up a common belief (bats rely almost entirely on echolocation) and reports a finding against it: the bats also used sight. The first and last choices contradict the text, since the bats did use echolocation in very low light. The text never compares bats with other animals.

Information and Ideas · 8 min

Command of evidence: textual

The text states a claim or hypothesis. You choose the finding that would support it, the finding that would weaken it, or, for a poem or novel, the quotation that best illustrates a claim about it.

Every choice is on topic. Only one connects to the exact claim.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which finding, if true, would most directly support the researchers' hypothesis?
  • Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the claim?
  • Which quotation from the poem most effectively illustrates the claim?

How to do it

  1. Find the claim and break it into its parts. A hypothesis usually links a cause to an effect: “cool river water → reefs survive heat”.
  2. Predict the evidence. What result would show that link? For “weaken”, what result would break it?
  3. Test each choice against every part of the claim. Evidence that touches only one part doesn't support the whole claim.
  4. For quotations, check the claim's key words. A claim about “both relief and regret” needs a line showing both.

Traps to avoid

Same topic, no link
A true-sounding fact about the subject that doesn't touch the cause and effect the claim is about.
A different explanation
The finding supports some other reason for the result, not the one the claim gives.
Half the claim
The quotation shows relief but not regret, or the finding shows the effect but not the cause.
Reversed
Read the question carefully: a choice that supports the claim is wrong when you're asked to weaken it.

Worked example

Example
Marine biologist Kofi Mensah hypothesizes that coral reefs near river mouths hold up better during ocean heat waves because cooler river water flowing over them lowers the temperature around the corals.

Which finding, if true, would most directly support Mensah's hypothesis?

  1. A

    A. Reefs near river mouths are home to more fish species than other reefs.

    On topic, but it says nothing about heat or river water.

  2. B

    B. During a recent heat wave, the water over reefs near river mouths stayed cooler than the water over other reefs, and fewer of their corals bleached. Answer

    Correct. It shows both parts of the hypothesis: the water stayed cooler, and the corals held up better.

  3. C

    C. Rivers carry sediment that can block sunlight from reaching corals.

    This suggests a different reason (shade from sediment), not cooler water.

  4. D

    D. Many reefs far from rivers survived the recent heat wave.

    This doesn't compare reefs near rivers with reefs far from them, so it can't support the hypothesis.

How to think about it

Claim: cooler river water → corals survive heat. The answer needs both the cooler water and the better survival.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Researchers hypothesized that students remember new vocabulary better when they study right before sleeping than when they study in the morning, because sleep strengthens newly formed memories.

Which finding, if true, would most directly weaken the researchers' hypothesis?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. The hypothesis credits sleep. If evening studiers did just as well without sleeping, sleep isn't what helps, so the hypothesis is weakened. The first choice supports it. Preferences and trouble falling asleep don't test whether sleep strengthens memory.

Quick check 2 of 2
In the poem “Harbor at Dusk,” the speaker describes the end of a fishing day with both relief and regret.

Which quotation from the poem most effectively illustrates the claim?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. “The long day's labor done” is relief; “and yet I watch the light go with a sigh” is regret. That's both parts of the claim. The other lines describe the harbor or the next day and show neither feeling clearly. (The poem is invented for this lesson.)

Information and Ideas · 8 min

Command of evidence: quantitative

These questions pair a short text with a table or graph. You choose the choice that uses the data to support a claim, complete an example, or describe a result.

Usually two or three choices are accurate readings of the graphic. Only one is accurate and fits the claim.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice most effectively uses data from the table to complete the statement?
  • Which choice best describes data from the graph that support the researcher's claim?

How to do it

  1. Read the text first. Find the claim the data are supposed to support. Underline exactly what it compares.
  2. Then read the graphic: its title, row and column labels, and units.
  3. Find the numbers the claim is about: which groups, which years, which measure.
  4. Check each choice twice. Is it true to the graphic? Does it support this claim? The answer must pass both checks.

Traps to avoid

Accurate but off-claim
A correct reading of the table that doesn't show what the claim says.
The wrong comparison
The claim compares A with B; the choice compares A with C.
Misread labels
The wrong row, column, year or unit.
Beyond the data
Tables show amounts, not causes. A choice that explains why the numbers changed goes beyond the data.

A group of students compared how well different mulches keep garden soil moist. They concluded that organic mulches, which are made from plant material, worked better than gravel. For example, ______

    A. soil with no mulch had a moisture level of 12%, while soil under gravel had a moisture level of 15%.

    Accurate, but neither number is for an organic mulch.

  2. B

    B. soil under straw had a moisture level of 27%, while soil under gravel had a moisture level of 15%. Answer

    Correct. Straw is an organic mulch, and it beats gravel, 27% to 15%.

  3. C

    Accurate, but it compares two organic mulches. Gravel isn't in it.

  4. D

    Accurate, but it says nothing about organic mulch.

How to think about it

The claim compares organic mulch with gravel. The answer needs straw or wood chips on one side and gravel on the other.

Information and Ideas · 8 min

Inferences

The text lays out a few facts and ends with a blank. You choose the conclusion that follows logically from those facts.

Inference doesn't mean guessing. The right answer is the smallest step the facts force you to take. It must be true if the text is true.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice most logically completes the text?

How to do it

  1. Read every sentence and note what each one establishes. Most texts set up two or three facts that combine.
  2. Notice the limits: one forest, one study, adults only, only in summer.
  3. Predict the conclusion those facts lead to, before you look at the choices.
  4. Pick the choice that must be true, not one that only might be. If a choice needs an extra fact the text didn't give, cross it out.

Traps to avoid

Goes too far
The text shows that something is likely; the choice says it's certain, or turns it into a policy recommendation.
Ignores a limit
The study looked at one city; the choice talks about every city.
Plausible but unsupported
It sounds reasonable in real life, but nothing in the text leads there.
Contradicts a detail
Read closely: one word in the text can rule a choice out.

Worked example

Example
Ecologists studying a forest found that a certain beetle lays its eggs only in trees that have recently been damaged by fire. They also found that, after decades of fire prevention, few trees in the forest had been burned recently. These findings suggest that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

  1. A

    A. the beetle population in the forest is probably smaller than it would be if fires were more frequent. Answer

    Correct. Fact 1: the beetles need burned trees. Fact 2: few trees have burned. Together: fewer places to lay eggs, so probably fewer beetles.

  2. B

    B. the forest's fire-prevention program should be ended.

    Too far. A finding about beetles doesn't decide what the forest's policy should be.

  3. C

    C. the beetle damages trees that have been burned.

    The text says where the beetle lays eggs, not that it damages the trees.

  4. D

    D. the beetle can be found only in this forest.

    The text studies one forest; it never says the beetle lives nowhere else.

How to think about it

Combine the two facts: the beetle needs burned trees, and burned trees are rare. The conclusion: fewer beetles.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
A museum curator noticed that a painting labeled as completed in 1820 contains a blue pigment that, according to chemical records, was first manufactured in 1834. Because the pigment appears in the painting's original layers of paint rather than in later repairs, the curator concluded that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. The pigment didn't exist until 1834, and it's in the original paint, not in a repair. So the painting couldn't have been finished before 1834, and the 1820 label is likely wrong. Nothing supports an 1820 repair or the artist inventing the pigment, and the curator's reasoning trusts the records.

Quick check 2 of 2
After the city added bike lanes to Elm Street, the number of cyclists on Elm Street rose by about 40 percent. During the same months, however, the number of cyclists on nearby Oak Street, which got no new lanes, fell by almost the same number. This suggests that ______

Which choice most logically completes the text?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Elm gained about as many cyclists as Oak lost, so the likeliest explanation is that riders moved over, not that new people started cycling. That also rules out a citywide 40 percent rise. Nothing in the text shows lanes discourage cycling or compares safety.

Standard English Conventions · 9 min

Boundaries

If the four choices use the same words and differ only in punctuation, it's a Boundaries question. You're deciding where one sentence ends and the next begins, and how to set off extra information.

A few rules cover almost every question. Learn the table below and these become some of the fastest points on the test.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

How to do it

  1. Check what changes across the choices. If only the punctuation changes, use these rules.
  2. Test both sides of the blank. Could the words before it stand alone as a sentence? Could the words after it? If both can, you need a period, a semicolon, or a comma with and, but or so.
  3. Look for extra information in the middle of a sentence. It needs a matching pair: comma and comma, dash and dash, or parentheses.
  4. Before a list or an explanation, use a colon, but only after a complete sentence.
  5. No reason to pause? Use no punctuation. Never put one mark between a subject and its verb, or between a verb and its object.

Traps to avoid

Comma splice
Two complete sentences joined by a comma alone. This is the most common wrong answer.
Mismatched pair
Extra information that opens with a dash and closes with a comma.
Colon after a fragment
“The causes were: heat and wind” is wrong. The words before a colon must be a complete sentence.
Splitting the subject from its verb
“The scientists who led the study, found…” The comma doesn't belong there.
However between two sentences
It needs a semicolon or period before it: “…was late; however, it…”

Punctuation rules

SituationUseExample
Two complete sentencesPeriod, semicolon, or comma + and/but/soThe lab closed; the study ended.
The second sentence explains the firstColon also worksThe lab closed: its funding had run out.
Extra information mid-sentenceMatching commas, dashes or parenthesesRuiz, a chemist, led it.
List or explanation after a complete sentenceColon (or dash)They tested three metals: tin, zinc and lead.
Subject → verb, verb → objectNothingThe results showed a clear pattern.

Worked example

Example
The Atacama Desert in Chile is one of the driest places on ______ some of its weather stations have gone years without recording any rain.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  1. A

    A. Earth,

    A comma splice. Both sides are complete sentences, so a comma alone can't join them.

  2. B

    B. Earth; Answer

    Correct. A semicolon joins two complete sentences.

  3. C

    C. Earth

    A run-on. Two complete sentences with nothing between them.

  4. D

    D. Earth, which

    “which some of its weather stations have gone years…” doesn't make sense as a clause.

How to think about it

Test both sides: “The Atacama Desert … is one of the driest places on Earth” is a sentence. “Some of its weather stations have gone years without … rain” is a sentence too. Two sentences need a period or semicolon.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Mary Lou Williams______ wrote and arranged music for some of the best-known jazz bands of the 1930s and 1940s.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. “A pianist from Pittsburgh” is extra information about Williams, so it needs a matching pair around it: a comma before and a comma after. The second choice opens the pair but doesn't close it. The third opens with a dash and closes with a comma. The fourth has the closing comma but no opening one.

Quick check 2 of 2
The team's report listed three causes of the power ______ an overloaded transformer, a damaged cable and a backup switch that failed.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. “The team's report listed three causes of the power outage” is a complete sentence, and a list follows, which is exactly what a colon is for. A comma makes the list look like more items in a series. A semicolon needs a complete sentence after it, and the list isn't one. “Including an overloaded transformer…” is a fragment.

Standard English Conventions · 9 min

Form, structure, and sense

If the choices change a word's form (describe or describes, its or their, who does the action after an opening phrase), it's a Form, structure, and sense question.

Like Boundaries, it rewards a short checklist. Figure out what's changing, then apply the one rule for it.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

How to do it

  1. See what changes across the choices: a verb, a pronoun, an apostrophe, or the order of a sentence.
  2. Verbs: find the real subject. Skip phrases like “of old letters” or “which the historian found”. Then match singular or plural, and keep the tense consistent with the verbs around it.
  3. Pronouns: find the noun the pronoun stands for, then match it. One company is it; two companies are they.
  4. Modifiers: after an opening phrase like “After studying the photos,” the next words must name who did the studying.
  5. Possessives: its means “belonging to it”; it's means “it is”. Same with their and they're.

Traps to avoid

The nearest noun
“The collection of letters describe…” The verb agrees with collection, not letters.
Group nouns
Team, committee, company and city are singular: use it and its.
Dangling modifier
“After studying the photos, a comet was found.” The comet didn't study anything.
Tense shift
The rest of the text is in the past tense; the choice switches to the present for no reason.

Quick checks

What changesAsk
Verb ending (is/are, -s/no -s)What is the real subject? One or many?
Verb tenseWhen does this happen? What tense are the nearby verbs?
Pronoun (it/they, its/their)Which noun does it replace? One or many?
Apostrophe (its/it's, -'s/-s')Is it possession or a contraction? One owner or many?
Word order after an opening phraseWho is doing the action in the opening phrase?

Worked example

Example
The collection of handwritten recipes, which a historian found in an attic in Ohio, ______ dishes that were popular in the region more than a century ago.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

  1. A

    A. describe

    Plural. The subject, collection, is singular.

  2. B

    B. describes Answer

    Correct. Cross out “of handwritten recipes” and the “which…” clause: “The collection describes dishes.”

  3. C

    C. are describing

    Plural, so it doesn't agree with collection.

  4. D

    D. have described

    Plural, so it doesn't agree with collection.

How to think about it

Three choices are plural and one is singular, so the question is about agreement. Strip away the extras to find the subject: collection.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
After studying thousands of photographs of the night sky, ______

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. Whoever studied the photographs must come right after the comma. That's the astronomer. Every other choice puts the comet or the discovery there, as if it did the studying.

Quick check 2 of 2
The city's oldest bakery still bakes bread in the same brick oven that ______ founders built in 1902.

Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. The founders belong to the bakery, which is singular: its founders. It's means “it is”. Their would need a plural noun to refer to, and they're means “they are”.

Expression of Ideas · 7 min

Rhetorical synthesis

You get a student's research notes as a bulleted list, and a goal, such as “emphasize a difference” or “introduce the study to an unfamiliar audience”. You choose the sentence that uses the notes to meet that goal.

Here's the key: every choice is accurate to the notes. The only thing that separates them is whether they meet the goal. Read the goal first, and you can often answer without reading the notes closely.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes: … The student wants to [goal]. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

How to do it

  1. Read the goal first. It's the last sentence before the question. Underline its key words: emphasize a similarity, explain a method, present the finding, for an audience unfamiliar with.
  2. Skim the notes, just enough to know what's in them.
  3. Test each choice against the goal alone. Ask: does this sentence do exactly that?
  4. For an “unfamiliar audience”, the sentence must say who or what the subject is, not assume the reader already knows.

Traps to avoid

Accurate but off-goal
A true sentence from the notes that does something else, such as giving a date when the goal is to explain a purpose.
Half the goal
The goal names a difference between two things, and the choice describes only one of them.
Assumes the background
For an unfamiliar audience, a sentence that mentions “the vault” without saying what it is doesn't work.

Worked example

Example
While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
  • The Svalbard Global Seed Vault opened in 2008 on a Norwegian island in the Arctic.
  • It stores backup copies of seeds from crop collections around the world.
  • The seeds are kept at about −18 °C (0 °F).
  • In 2015, researchers withdrew seeds from the vault to rebuild a crop collection damaged during the war in Syria.

The student wants to explain the vault's purpose to an audience unfamiliar with it. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

  1. A

    A. The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, on an Arctic island in Norway, stores backup copies of seeds from crop collections around the world. Answer

    Correct. It names and places the vault for a new reader, and states its purpose: storing backup copies of the world's crop seeds.

  2. B

    B. The seeds in the Svalbard Global Seed Vault are kept at about −18 °C (0 °F).

    Accurate, but it's a storage detail, not the purpose.

  3. C

    C. In 2015, researchers withdrew seeds from the vault.

    It assumes the reader knows what “the vault” is, and it describes one event, not the purpose.

  4. D

    D. The vault opened in 2008, seven years before researchers withdrew seeds from it.

    Dates and timing, not purpose, and it also assumes the reader knows the vault.

How to think about it

Goal keywords: purpose and unfamiliar audience. The answer must say what the vault is and what it's for.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
  • Honeybees live in colonies of tens of thousands of bees.
  • Most bee species, such as the blue orchard bee, live alone.
  • A female blue orchard bee builds her own nest in a hollow stem or a hole in wood.
  • Both honeybees and blue orchard bees pollinate fruit trees.

The student wants to emphasize a difference between honeybees and blue orchard bees. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Only the first choice names both bees and contrasts them (“While…”). The second is a similarity. The third and fourth describe only the blue orchard bee.

Quick check 2 of 2
While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
  • Researcher Dana Okafor studied 120 apartment buildings in one city.
  • Some of the buildings had rooftop gardens; the others did not.
  • In summer, top-floor apartments in buildings with rooftop gardens were on average 3 °C cooler.
  • The gardens also gave residents a place to grow vegetables.

The student wants to present the main finding of Okafor's study. Which choice most effectively uses relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. A finding is a result, and the second choice states the result. The first describes the method, the third is a side note about the gardens, and the fourth says what the study looked at without saying what it found.

Expression of Ideas · 7 min

Transitions

A blank sits at the start of a sentence (or clause), and the choices are transition words like however, therefore and for example. You choose the one that matches how the ideas actually relate.

Decide on the relationship before you read the choices, and these go fast.

How to spot it

The question will read like this:

  • Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

How to do it

  1. Cover the choices.
  2. Read the sentence before the blank and the sentence with the blank. Sometimes you need the whole text.
  3. Name the relationship in plain words: “and also”, “but”, “so”, “for example”, “then”.
  4. Pick the transition that means that. If two choices mean the same thing (therefore and thus), neither one can be the single right answer.

Traps to avoid

Same-meaning pairs
If therefore and as a result both appear, cross out both. There's only one right answer.
Reading one sentence
The relationship is between two ideas, so read the sentence before the blank too.
Instead vs however
Instead means one thing replaces another. However just signals a contrast.
In fact vs for example
In fact strengthens or corrects the last point. For example gives a specific case of it.

Transitions by relationship

RelationshipTransitions
Adds a pointfurthermore, moreover, in addition, additionally, also
Shows a similaritysimilarly, likewise
Shows a contrasthowever, by contrast, nevertheless, still, on the other hand
Replaces one idea with anotherinstead, rather
Shows a resulttherefore, thus, as a result, consequently, hence
Gives an examplefor example, for instance, specifically
Shows time orderfirst, then, later, subsequently, finally
Admits a pointgranted, admittedly, of course
Emphasizes or restatesin fact, indeed, in other words, that is

Worked example

Example
For years, the town's river was too polluted for swimming. In 2015, a new water treatment plant began operating upstream. ______ by 2020, the river was clean enough to host a summer swimming race.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

  1. A

    A. As a result, Answer

    Correct. The plant came first, and the cleaner river followed from it: a result.

  2. B

    B. However,

    A contrast, but the cleaner river is what you'd expect after a new treatment plant, not a surprise.

  3. C

    C. For example,

    The swimming race isn't an example of the plant; it's an outcome.

  4. D

    D. Similarly,

    Nothing here is being compared.

How to think about it

Plain words: “new plant… so clean river.” “So” means a result.

Try it

Quick check 1 of 2
Most spiders catch prey in webs that they rebuild every day or two. The bolas spider, ______ spins no web at all; it swings a single sticky thread at passing moths.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Show the explanation

Answer: B. Most spiders build webs, and the bolas spider doesn't: a contrast. It isn't an example of web-building spiders, a result of anything, or an added point of the same kind.

Quick check 2 of 2
Octopuses can change the color of their skin in a fraction of a second. ______ many species can change the texture of their skin, raising small bumps that make them look like rock or coral.

Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?

Show the explanation

Answer: A. Changing texture is a second, separate ability added to changing color: moreover. Nothing contrasts or is replaced, so nevertheless and instead don't fit, and the texture change isn't caused by the color change, so thus doesn't fit either.
